## Doll of the Fallen Soldier



I don't remember the name of the field. That's the first thing people don't understand about war: you remember the mud, the smell, the sound a body makes when it stops being a person and starts being weight. You don't remember the name. We'd held the line since dawn. I found the doll after the battle was over. Not won. 



The fighting had moved east before sunset, leaving the company behind to count the living, strip anything useful from the dead, and burn what could not be carried. I had done it before. Weapons first. Ammunition second. Boots if the soles were good. Identification last, assuming the rain had not washed away the ink or the blood had not ruined it. There was nothing noble about battlefield recovery. It was inventory performed on corpses.



The soldier lay beneath the remains of a shattered wagon, one arm trapped under the axle and the other curled tightly against his chest. Young. Younger than I like to think about. His armor was cheap, the kind a family scrapes together, not the kind a kingdom issues. He wore the enemy’s colors, though mud had reduced both armies to the same brown-red uniform. His sword was missing. His armor had been split beneath the ribs. He had probably lived for several minutes after the line advanced.  I don't know if I put him down. I want to say I'd remember that. I want to say a kill leaves a mark you can find later, like a notch cut into something. It doesn't. Not in a field with forty bodies and no more light left to see faces by.



I searched him because that was the work.



Coins. Flint. A whetstone worn nearly smooth. A folded piece of paper so soaked that it came apart between my fingers.

His hand was closed around something. The soldier had been holding it so tightly that I had to pull his fingers loose one at a time.



A doll.

The kind of thing a father makes for a child. It was small enough to hide in one hand. Rough cloth, dark yarn for hair, two mismatched buttons for eyes. One leg had been repaired with blue thread. Its tiny coat had been stitched from the same wool used in army blankets.



I don't know why I kept it. I told myself I took it because there might be a name sewn inside.



There was not.



&#x20;I told myself someone might recognize it.



No one did.



I told myself I would turn it over to the quartermaster.



I never did.



I don't know why I kept it. I've thought about that more than I'd like to admit. Souvenirs are for people who want to remember. I've spent most of my life trying to do the opposite. Someone had made it for him, or he had made it for someone else. Someone had chosen the buttons. Someone had repaired the torn leg rather than replace the whole thing. Somewhere beyond the battlefield, there may have been a person waiting for a soldier who would never return and a little cloth figure that had vanished with him. But my hand closed around it before my head decided anything, and by the time I noticed, it was already in my pocket, and taking it back out and leaving it on his chest felt like a second violation. So I carried him instead. The only piece of him I could carry without breaking something in myself.



It weighed nothing.



It took up almost no room.



Throwing it away would not bring him back.



All true.



It rode in my pack for longer than I can count. Through postings I don't talk about. Through the parts of my life before this one, the parts that don't come up unless someone asks the wrong question at the wrong hour. I hid it from the drow the same as I hid everything else that mattered, the same instinct that keeps a blade sheathed until it's needed. Nobody takes what they don't know you have.

I told myself it was nothing. A scrap. Weight I could afford to carry because it didn't slow me down.

It was never nothing. I know that now, because I've felt it move.



I've felt it grow warm in my hand when I had no right to still be breathing. I've seen it stand up on legs that shouldn't exist, wearing a face I recognized and didn't want to. Whatever that boy was, whatever I took from him on a field I can't name, some of it is still in there. Watching. Waiting for the moment I go down and can't get back up on my own. That day came.



Years later, in the Feydark, the dead came back.



Not their bodies. That would have been easier.



They came back standing.



The field stretched in every direction beneath a sky with no sun. Soldiers rose from the mud wearing every uniform I had ever fought beside or against. Some bore wounds that should have dropped them. Others had no wounds at all. Those were worse. Those looked as they might have looked before I reached them.



They closed around my.



I swung.



My fist passed through one soldier’s jaw as though he were smoke. Another seized my arm. A third whispered something I could not hear. The battlefield shifted beneath my feet, cycling through every place where I had followed an order because following it had been easier than deciding whether it was right.



Then I saw the doll lying in the mud.



It was clean.



The blue thread around its leg shone faintly beneath the dark sky.



I reached for it, but the soldiers moved between us. I struck, shoved, and fought forward. Each time I gained ground, the field stretched farther. Every tactical instinct I possessed became useless. There was no formation to break. No commander to kill. No route of retreat.



Only the doll.



Only the dead.



Only the certainty that I could not reach all of them.



I stopped fighting.



The soldiers stopped.



I looked at the nearest one. I couldn't tell whether  he had been an ally or an enemy. I couldn't remember whether I had killed him, failed to save him, or merely stepped over him.



“I don’t know your name,” I said.



The words felt pathetic. Insufficient.



The soldier waited.



I looked past him at the others.



“I don’t know any of your names.”



No one answered.



“I can’t carry all of you.”



That was the truth I had spent years avoiding. Not because I feared the dead, but because some part of me believed remembering them badly was another kind of failure.



The soldiers began to fade.



The last was the one beneath the wagon.



His face remained indistinct, no matter how hard I tried to see it. He knelt, lifted the doll from the mud, and held it out.



This time, I did not take it from a dead man’s hand.



I accepted it from someone giving it to me.



When I woke, the doll was clenched against my chest.



The blue repair thread now glimmered like a narrow line of moonlight. The mismatched button eyes reflected light that was not present in the cavern. Beneath its little coat, where I had searched countless times for a name, new words had appeared in tiny, uneven stitches:



CARRY THE LIVING.



I stared at them for a long time.



The doll had not awakened to demand vengeance. It did not contain the dead soldier’s soul or whisper his forgotten name. It offered no absolution, because absolution was not something a soldier could loot from a battlefield.



It had become a reminder with a purpose.



The dead were beyond my reach.



The living were not.



I don't understand forgiveness. I've never been good at receiving it, and I'm worse at asking for it. But something made of dead wood and a dead boy's hands keeps choosing to catch me instead of letting me fall, and I don't have a tactical explanation for that. I only have the fact of it.



Carry the living.



So I do.



I carry him too. I probably always will.
